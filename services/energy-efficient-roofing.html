Where Roof Energy Performance Actually Comes From

Katy's summer sun pushes untreated roof surfaces past 150°F, and that heat pours straight into the attic and radiates down into the house overnight. An energy-efficient approach attacks it in three layers. Surface: light and "cool" shingle colors with solar-reflective granule technology bounce more sunlight before it becomes heat; bare and coated metal systems perform even better. Air: balanced intake at the soffits and exhaust at the ridge keep attic air moving, so heat doesn't bank all day and moisture from our humid Gulf air doesn't condense on cold decking. Sealing: can lights, top plates, and attic hatches leak conditioned air into the attic year-round; a roof project is the cheapest moment to address them.

None of this is exotic — it's decisions made correctly, at the exact moment you're already paying for materials and labor.

Materials That Earn Their Keep in Katy Summers

Cool asphalt shingles. Manufacturer lines with reflective granules now come in the same earthy palettes Katy homeowners actually want — weathered wood, slate, bark tones — while reflecting noticeably more solar energy than standard versions of the same color. Standing seam and coated metal. Reflective pigment systems, minimal heat retention, and decades of service life; on a West or South-facing exposure with full sun, the difference is measurable in attic temperature. Tile. Clay and concrete hold heat but the air channel beneath the courses acts as a natural thermal break on homes built for it. Coatings. On low-slope and commercial roofs, reflective elastomeric and silicone coatings drop surface temperatures dramatically and extend the life of the membrane underneath.

Which combination pays off for your specific roof depends on orientation, shading, attic design, and your HVAC situation — which is what the assessment is for.

Ventilation: The Half That Gets Skipped

Almost every builder-grade Katy roof we open has the same quiet flaw: ridge vents without matching soffit intake, or an attic choked by insulation blocking the air path. Exhaust without intake does almost nothing — the system can't move air it can't draw.

We measure the actual net free vent area, check for blocked baffles and crushed soffit vents, and size intake and exhaust to the attic's real volume. Proper airflow does two jobs in this climate: it carries heat out before it saturates the attic, and it keeps humidity from condensing and quietly rotting decking from the underside — a Katy-specific problem that ventilation failures turn expensive.
