Why Metal Makes Sense in This Climate

Hail is the argument-ender. Class 4 impact-rated metal takes a Katy hailstorm that would send a shingle roof to the insurance adjuster and mostly just needs an inspection after. Heat: reflective pigment coatings shed solar energy that asphalt absorbs, so the roof surface and the attic below both run cooler. Wind: properly fastened standing seam, with concealed clips and no exposed fasteners to back out, holds wind ratings that shingles approach only in their best product lines. Fire and water: metal is non-combustible and sheds water fast, with no granules to lose and no curling tabs for wind to catch.

Service life is the quiet payoff: metal roofs routinely run two to three times the life of a shingle roof. The upfront number is higher; the math per year of roof is where it wins — especially if you're staying in the home long-term.

Metal Roof Styles We Install

Standing seam — the premium system: vertical panels with concealed fasteners and floating clips that let the metal expand and contract with Texas temperature swings. Nothing exposed to back out, nothing to caulk every few years; the right choice for a forever roof. Exposed-fastener panels — corrugated and ribbed panels installed with screws and washer seals; an economical metal option for workshops, barns, porches, and outbuildings around Katy acreage, with honest maintenance expectations (those fasteners get re-sealed on a schedule). Metal shingles and tile profiles — stamped panels that mimic slate, shake, or barrel tile for homes where the HOA or the architecture asks for a traditional look with metal performance underneath.

Steel with galvalume or zinc-aluminum coating is the Katy workhorse; aluminum earns its place near the coast; the paint system — Kynar-class finishes — is what actually determines whether the color you pick still looks right in year fifteen.

Installation Is Everything on Metal

Metal roofing has a particular failure mode: it's unforgiving of shortcut installation. Under-fastened panels sing in the wind. Clips spaced to save material telegraph waves across the roof. Dissimilar metals in contact corrode each other. A crew unfamiliar with the details — valley trimming, transitions, fastener torque — creates problems that show up in the first big storm.

We install metal the way the manufacturer's handbook says to: correct underlayment (high-temp synthetic, not the organic felt that bakes under metal), correct clip spacing, correct trim details, and correct fasteners — every time, not just where it's visible from the ground.
