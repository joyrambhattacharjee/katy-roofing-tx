Texas Heat Is Eating Your Roof From Below

Everyone blames the sun. But on a July afternoon in Katy, the surface of your shingles is the cool side of the roof — the underside, against an attic cooking at 130-plus degrees, is where your roof actually ages.

Published October 2026 · Texas heat guide

The Bake, Explained

A shingle roof is built to take sun from above. It is not built to take heat from below, and that's what a poorly vented attic delivers. Sun beats on the roof, the roof deck heats up, and the attic beneath becomes an oven with nowhere to send the heat. That trapped heat soaks through the deck and cooks the shingle mat and its asphalt from the underside — softening the oils out of it, curling edges, and stripping years of life the manufacturer promised. Two roofs with identical materials, one over a ventilated attic and one over a sealed box, can age a decade apart in greater Katy.

How Ventilation Actually Works

Good attic ventilation isn't complicated physics — it's a chimney effect. Cool air enters low (at the soffit vents under your eaves), warm air exits high (at the ridge vent along the peak or through static vents near the top). The moving air carries heat and moisture out all day. What breaks the system is predictable: soffit vents crushed by attic-stored boxes or painted over; insulation shoved against the roof deck that blocks the air path from the eaves; ridge vents that were installed but never actually cut open underneath; and bathroom exhaust fans that dump humid air into the attic instead of through the roof — which adds moisture that condenses on the deck at night and rots it slowly.

What a Sick Attic Looks Like

Climb up (or look at photos from an inspection) on a summer afternoon and check for the tells: a thermometer reading far above the outdoor temperature; decking that's darkened or stained with moisture marks, especially near the peak; shingles whose edges are curling up like a wave about to break; upstairs rooms that stay stubbornly warmer than downstairs; and an AC system that runs all afternoon in July. Any two of those together usually mean the ventilation isn't doing its job.

The Fixes, From Cheap to Worth-Bundling

Some ventilation corrections cost nearly nothing: clearing soffit intakes, pulling stored items off the eave lines, fixing that bathroom fan routing. Others are modest projects — opening or adding ridge vents, adding static vents when ridge venting isn't practical, installing proper baffles that keep insulation from blocking the air path. The moment to take the expensive ones seriously is during a roof replacement, when the deck is open and the whole ventilation system can be designed correctly for once — intake, exhaust, and the channel between them. Bundling ventilation work into a replacement is one of the few upgrades that reliably extends the new roof's life rather than just decorating it.

What This Means for Your Next Roof Decisions

If you're repairing: check the attic before assuming the shingles alone are the problem, because heat damage mimics storm damage. If you're replacing: this is the time to get ventilation right — and to weigh material choices with heat in mind, which our energy efficient roofing page covers in detail, from reflective shingle colors to metal and the ventilation pairing that makes any of them perform. If you're in a newer Katy-area neighborhood with a production-built roof, know that ventilation shortcuts are among the most common things we document — a ridge vent "installed" over solid decking is a classic. The Elyson area page covers what we typically find on new construction.

Catch It Early

Attic heat damage is visible before it's catastrophic — curling shingles, stained decking, upstairs rooms that won't cool. An inspection that includes the attic, not just the roof, catches it.
