Repair or Replace? Reading Your Roof Honestly

Every roof eventually asks the same question. Here's how to weigh the answer — and how to tell whether the bid you're holding serves your roof or the contractor's calendar.

Published October 2026 · Big decisions guide

The Two Honest Questions

Strip away the sales language and every repair-or-replace decision comes down to two questions. One: is the problem isolated or systemic? A cracked pipe boot is isolated. A 22-year-old shingle field losing granules into the gutters is systemic. Two: how much useful life does the roof have left? A repair buys time; it only makes sense when there's meaningful time left to buy.

Signs the Repair Path Still Works

Repairs earn their place when the roof is fundamentally sound. You're likely a repair candidate if: the roof is under 12–15 years old; the leak traces to one identifiable spot — a pipe boot, a chimney flashing, a valley, a punctured boot from an HVAC tech; shingles are flat, sealed, and holding their granules; and past repairs, if any, have held. Isolated failures on a sound roof are the bread and butter of good repair work, and a documented repair can add years for a fraction of replacement cost. Our roof repair page covers what we fix most and how.

Signs the Roof Is Telling You It's Done

Systemic age shows up in patterns, not single spots: recurring leaks in new places after each repair; granules filling gutter runs and washing out at downspouts after every rain; shingles that are cupping, clawing, or creasing; bare-looking slopes where the grit has worn off the south and west faces; neighbors on the same street — same builder, same year — replacing theirs. When three or more of those line up, the repair math flips: you're renting patches on a roof that's already left, and every repair dollar buys less time than the last one.

The Math, Without Numbers

We don't publish prices — every roof is quoted after inspection, honestly, based on what's actually up there. But the shape of the decision is universal: a repair costs a fraction of a replacement and should buy a meaningful fraction of a replacement's remaining life. When a repair is priced at, say, a third of replacement but the roof has only a year or two left, you're paying a third for a tenth — the wrong trade. When a small repair buys five dry years on an otherwise sound roof, nothing else makes sense. Ask any bidder to frame their recommendation that way; a good one will do it unprompted.

The Steering Problem

Some contractors steer everyone toward replacement because replacement pays more. Some steer everyone toward repairs to close fast and cheap. You're on solid ground when the recommendation comes with photos of your actual roof showing why — the creased shingle, the failed field, or the intact slope around the one bad boot. That's the standard we hold ourselves to, and it's the standard to hold anyone to.

Katy's Version of the Question

Roofs age on Texas time. Hail seasons stack damage that doesn't leak today; Gulf humidity and attic heat cook shingles from both sides. That's why a 20-year-old Katy roof is often closer to done than a 20-year-old roof somewhere milder, and why the neighborhoods built in the same wave — a lot of Cinco Ranch and Seven Meadows streets, for example — face the same decision in the same years.
